On the teaching market, 2026–27

I write about criminal law and procedure, with particular attention to the institutions that are supposed to make the system work — and what happens to them when they come under strain.

My current project, Holistic Defense, Reconsidered, forthcoming in the Washington University Law Review, examines the now-widespread practice of embedding social workers in public defender offices. The model is intuitive and appealing, but I argue it carries three underappreciated risks: it can widen the net of criminal legal control rather than narrow it, it is structurally ill-suited to delivering benefits that outlast a criminal case, and it directs scarce social services toward people charged with crimes rather than the wider community that may need them just as much. I don't conclude that offices should abandon the model. I propose practices to mitigate those risks — and, more ambitiously, an inverted institutional structure in which criminal defense sits inside a social services organization rather than the reverse.

My other work takes up the interstate machinery of extradition, the ways the criminal legal system can be attacked through its resource constraints, and the design of executive clemency. Some of it draws on behavioral science, a running interest since before law school.

Before joining the Quattrone Center, I spent three years as a staff attorney at Queens Defenders (now Brooklyn Defender Services), where I represented misdemeanor and felony clients from arraignment through trial and built the discovery software the office still runs on. Earlier, I worked at Cornerstone Research on economic expert testimony in complex civil litigation. I'm a graduate of Harvard Law School, where I was editor-in-chief of the Harvard Negotiation Law Review, and of Penn, where I studied philosophy, politics, and economics alongside Hispanic studies.
